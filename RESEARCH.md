# Research basis for the GLORB Earth Signal Calibration

## Scope

This resource is designed as a student self-report, communication and collaborative planning tool. It is not presented as a validated clinical diagnostic instrument.

## Design decisions supported by research

### 1. Emotional awareness plus self-management
The question sequence asks students to identify feelings, body/thinking cues, observable behaviour, context and helpful responses. This is consistent with CASEL's self-awareness and self-management competencies and the Australian Curriculum V9 Personal and Social Capability focus on emotional regulation.

Sources:
- CASEL, What Is the CASEL Framework? https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/what-is-the-casel-framework/
- Australian Curriculum V9, Personal and Social Capability. https://v9.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-general-capability/personal-and-social-capability

### 2. Explicit school-based teaching of emotion regulation
A systematic review of 36 school-based adolescent emotion-regulation intervention studies reported small to moderate effects for mental-health and emotion-regulation outcomes overall, with variability across programs and populations. This supports explicit teaching and planning while cautioning against claiming that one brief tool will produce a fixed outcome.

Source:
- Pedrini L, Meloni S, Lanfredi M, Rossi R. School-based interventions to improve emotional regulation skills in adolescent students: A systematic review. Journal of Adolescence. 2022;94(8):1051-1067. doi:10.1002/jad.12090

### 3. Strategy flexibility rather than a universal calming prescription
Contemporary emotion-regulation research increasingly emphasises flexibility: the usefulness of a strategy depends on the person, goal and context. This is why the build says "Choose strategies that actually help you" and prints "student has identified" rather than declaring a strategy to be correct for a signal.

Source:
- Haag A-C et al. Emotion Regulation Flexibility in Adolescents: A Systematic Review from Conceptualization to Methodology. Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review. 2024. doi:10.1007/s10567-024-00483-6

### 4. Slow breathing is optional, not compulsory
Relaxation-based interventions have evidence for reducing anxiety and distress in adolescents and young adults, but effect sizes vary by method and population. A recent adolescent randomized trial of resonant breathing found clear physiological effects but no overall differences in affect or recovery. The build therefore offers slow, comfortable breathing only as one option and uses the phrase "if it helps me".

Sources:
- Hamdani SU et al. Effectiveness of relaxation techniques as an active ingredient of psychological interventions to reduce distress, anxiety and depression in adolescents: a systematic review and meta-analysis. International Journal of Mental Health Systems. 2022. doi:10.1186/s13033-022-00541-y
- Michels N et al. The Immediate Effects of a Resonant Breathing Exercise on Adolescents' Stress Responses: A Randomized Trial. Biopsychosocial Science and Medicine. 2026;88(2):166-178. doi:10.1097/PSY.0000000000001448

### 5. Body-signal questions are used as self-observation, not as a diagnostic claim
Interoception is relevant to emotion research, but adolescent findings are not simple or uniformly positive. Recent adolescent research has found mixed associations between interoceptive measures and emotion regulation. The build therefore asks "What do you notice?" rather than claiming that a body cue proves a particular emotion or that body awareness itself guarantees regulation improvement.

Sources:
- Braet J et al. Should Adolescents Listen to Their Hearts? A Closer Look at the Associations Between Interoception, Emotional Awareness and Emotion Regulation in Adolescents. Psychological Reports. 2026;129(4):3237-3254. doi:10.1177/00332941241286435
- Braet J, Braet C. I can feel my heartbeat: The relationship between interoceptive abilities and emotional states during stress and recovery in healthy adolescents. Psychophysiology. 2024;61(12):e14679. doi:10.1111/psyp.14679

### 6. Adult support and co-regulation remain important
Emotion regulation develops in relationships. Developmental reviews describe co-regulation as external support and scaffolding that helps children manage challenging emotional states and develop regulation skills. Research on secondary-school teacher-student relationships also links supportive relationships with better emotional outcomes, although much of that evidence is correlational and school-specific co-regulation research is still developing.

Sources:
- Paley B, Hajal NJ. Conceptualizing Emotion Regulation and Coregulation as Family-Level Phenomena. Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review. 2022. doi:10.1007/s10567-022-00378-4
- Verhagen C et al. Coregulation between parents and elementary school-aged children in response to challenge and in association with child outcomes: A systematic review. Developmental Psychology. 2024. doi:10.1037/dev0001864
- Anxiety and Teacher-Student Relationships in Secondary School: A Systematic Literature Review. Child Psychiatry & Human Development. 2024. doi:10.1007/s10578-024-01665-7

## How to interpret the strategy bank

The strategy bank contains three different kinds of supports:

1. Research-supported regulation approaches, such as relaxation, slowing down, and adult support.
2. Common school access supports, such as a quieter space, fewer words, a break, task chunking and predictable choices.
3. Individual sensory or practical supports from the supplied Glorb framework, such as fidgets, movement, water/snack or strong-muscle activity.

The resource does not claim that every individual card has equal research support. Instead, it uses a flexible planning approach: the student identifies candidates, adults observe effectiveness in context, and the plan is updated collaboratively.

## Important implementation rule

Do not use this plan to infer intent, diagnose a condition, or replace an existing health, disability, behaviour or safety plan. If a selected strategy conflicts with school procedures or an individual plan, follow the established plan and adapt the Signal Plan accordingly.

## V3 student-facing architecture

The learner-facing version now begins with a small set of common school emotions rather than the Signal categories. This is a design choice, not a claim that the eight feeling labels form a validated emotion taxonomy. The purpose is to lower response burden and let the learner start from familiar language.

For each chosen feeling, the student reports body cues, action urges, situations that make the feeling bigger, self-selected strategies, helpful responses from other people, and responses that do not help. Signal labels are introduced only in the adult report.

The automated Signal grouping uses selected body and action patterns, not the emotion word itself. This prevents the tool from treating an emotion such as frustration as belonging to one fixed regulation state. The mapping should be interpreted as a planning hypothesis and reviewed with the student, not as a psychometric score or clinical classification.
